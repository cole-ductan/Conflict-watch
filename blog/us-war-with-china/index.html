US War with China: What the Buildup Actually Looks Like

Searches for a US war with China spike every time China holds drills around Taiwan. This post separates what is verified from what is speculation — using only named sources, with contested claims attributed and confidence labeled.

The direct answer: As of NOV 16, 2026, the US and China are not at war, and no named original source — Reuters, AP, the US Department of Defense, or an official statement from either government — has reported a decision by China to invade Taiwan or a timetable for doing so.

What is verified is a long-running military buildup on both sides: China has built the world’s largest navy by ship count and is expanding its nuclear arsenal, the PLA rehearses encircling and blockading Taiwan in large exercises, and the US is strengthening access and alliances across the Indo-Pacific. That is a competition in posture and readiness. It is not, on the verified record, a war starting.

1. What Is Verified About the Posture

China’s forces, per the Pentagon

  • Verified — US Department of Defense The Pentagon’s 2024 report to Congress, Military and Security Developments Involving the People’s Republic of China, assessed that China had more than 600 operational nuclear warheads by mid-2024, and projected China could have more than 1,000 by 2030. Those are Pentagon estimates, not a count China has confirmed.
  • Verified — US Department of Defense The same report described the PLA Navy as the largest navy in the world by number of ships, with more than 370 battle-force ships and submarines. Largest by ship count is not the same as largest by tonnage or capability — the Pentagon measures it by hulls.
  • Pentagon assessment The 2024 report also described China as having a leading hypersonic missile arsenal and a large, growing force of ballistic and cruise missiles. Capability assessments like these are US government judgments, labeled here as such.

Taiwan: the stated positions

  • Verified China claims Taiwan as part of its territory. Taiwan is governed democratically and separately. Those are the two stated positions, and neither side has changed them.
  • Verified — Congressional Research Service US policy is the “one-China” policy, guided by the Taiwan Relations Act, the three Joint Communiqués, and the Six Assurances. Under it, the US recognizes the People’s Republic of China as the sole legal government of China, maintains unofficial relations with Taiwan, and — under the Taiwan Relations Act — makes defensive arms available to Taiwan and maintains the capacity to resist coercion against it. The Act does not contain an automatic US commitment to fight in Taiwan’s defense; that ambiguity is deliberate and longstanding.

The drills around Taiwan

  • Verified — Reuters In late December 2025, China held “Justice Mission 2025,” which Reuters described as its most extensive war games around Taiwan to date and the sixth major round since 2022. Reuters reported live-fire exercises, simulated strikes, and drills rehearsing a blockade of Taiwan’s main ports, with forces operating closer to Taiwan than in previous rounds.
  • Verified — Reuters Reuters reported those drills began 11 days after the US announced an $11.1 billion arms package for Taiwan, which Reuters described as the largest ever for the island, and that China’s defence ministry protested it. The sequence is verified; whether the package caused the timing is Beijing’s and analysts’ interpretation, not a verified fact.
  • Reported — Taiwan’s defence ministry, via Reuters Taiwan’s defence ministry counts of Chinese aircraft and vessels operating around the island during drills are official Taiwanese figures reported by Reuters. Treat exact daily counts as attributed figures, not independently confirmed totals.

The US and allied posture

  • Verified In 2023, the Philippines and the US expanded agreed locations under the Enhanced Defense Cooperation Agreement (EDCA) from five to nine. EDCA sites are Philippine bases that remain under Philippine ownership and control; US forces use them on a rotational basis. They are not US-owned bases.
  • Verified — US Department of Defense The US and Australia have agreed to increase rotational presence of US forces in Australia, including bomber and fighter rotations, and to deepen logistics and industrial cooperation, per the Department of Defense.
  • Verified AUKUS — the Australia, UK, and US partnership announced in 2021, centered on nuclear-powered submarines for Australia and advanced technology sharing — is a real, signed program. Its submarines are a long-term project measured in years, not a near-term deployment.

2. What Is Speculation or Contested

  • The “2027” date. Contested interpretation Reuters has reported that a Pentagon report assessed China was preparing to be able to win a fight for Taiwan by 2027. A readiness goal is not an invasion date, and no named source has produced a Chinese decision or order to invade in 2027. Claims online that “China will invade in 2027” go beyond what the underlying assessment says.
  • Wargame results. Think-tank assessment — CSIS The Center for Strategic and International Studies ran a wargame of a Chinese invasion of Taiwan 24 times and published it in January 2023 as The First Battle of the Next War. In most iterations, the US, Taiwan, and Japan defeated the invasion, at very high cost in ships, aircraft, and personnel on all sides. A wargame is a model built on assumptions — it shows what could be costly, not what will happen.
  • Blockade vs. invasion vs. quarantine. Analysts disagree on which option China would choose if it moved on Taiwan, and PLA drills have rehearsed blockade elements. Which course Beijing would pick — if any — is speculation. No verified plan has been published by a named original source.
  • Exact PLA readiness and intent. Low confidence Estimates of how quickly China could mount a full invasion, and whether its leadership intends to, vary widely among named analysts and are not settled. Ship and warhead counts are measurable; intent is not.
  • Unverified Social-media claims of secret mobilization, imminent attack dates, or troops already moving for an invasion have no confirmation from Reuters, AP, the Pentagon, or official statements, and are not treated as fact here.

3. What Would Signal Real Escalation

None of the following is a routine drill announcement. These are the changes that would move this from posture to a genuine US war with China risk:

  • A sustained blockade or quarantine in practice — Chinese forces actually stopping or turning back commercial shipping to Taiwan over days, not rehearsing it in a timed exercise with announced end dates.
  • Civilian and economic mobilization in China — verified, large-scale call-ups, requisition of civilian shipping at scale, or blood and medical mobilization reported by named original sources. An invasion force cannot be hidden at that scale.
  • Evacuations and official warnings — the US or allies ordering dependents or citizens out of Taiwan or the region, or raising force-protection levels publicly, via official statements.
  • Missile and amphibious forces moving to staging — confirmed by the Pentagon or allied defence ministries, not by anonymous social-media video.
  • Diplomatic break signals — ambassadors recalled, direct military hotlines cut, or an ultimatum with a stated deadline from an official statement by either government.
  • Live fire that does not stop — exercises that cross into strikes on Taiwanese forces or territory, verified by Taiwan’s defence ministry and independent reporting.

4. What to Watch

  • The next Pentagon China Military Power Report — whether the warhead projection and PLA Navy count keep rising at the same pace.
  • The pattern of PLA exercises: size, closeness to Taiwan, whether blockade rehearsals expand, and whether drills end on schedule as announced.
  • US arms deliveries to Taiwan, not just announcements — packages are announced in Washington; deliveries are what change Taiwan’s actual defenses.
  • Whether the nine EDCA sites in the Philippines are built out and used for rotations, and any change to the terms the Philippines sets for their use.
  • Official language from both capitals: any shift from claiming Taiwan and opposing “unilateral changes” to deadlines, ultimatums, or evacuation advice.
  • Any US statement that changes the Taiwan Relations Act framework — that framework, not a headline, is what defines the US position.

Method: Conflict Ready USA tracks US threats only, attributes contested claims to who made them, labels confidence, and keeps unverified claims off the verified count. If a claim cannot be tied to a named original source, it is labeled unverified here — not repeated as fact.
