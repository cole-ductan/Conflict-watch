Conflict Tracker: How to Tell What's Real

A conflict tracker is only useful if you can trust what it shows you. This post explains how Conflict Ready USA decides what counts as verified, what stays labeled unverified, and how you can run the same checks yourself.

What this tracker covers — and what it leaves out

Conflict Ready USA covers only threats to the United States. That means:

  • US bases and US personnel
  • US treaty allies
  • US shipping and trade routes
  • US infrastructure and cyber threats

Anything else stays out. A narrower scope is deliberate. It keeps the feed focused on what could affect Americans directly, instead of trying to cover every conflict in the world.

How every report is verified

1. Checked against the original source

Every report is verified against its original source — Reuters, AP, CENTCOM, UKMTO, JMIC, or an official statement — not against an aggregator, a screenshot, or a repost. If the original source cannot be found, the report does not go in the main feed.

2. Every contested claim is attributed

If a claim is disputed, the report says who made it. Readers can see the source of the claim and judge it for themselves, instead of reading a contested statement presented as settled fact.

3. Each report carries a confidence label

Every report is labeled for confidence, so you can tell at a glance how solid the sourcing is before you act on it or share it.

4. Severity means threat to the US only

Severity on this site measures threat to the United States only. It is not a general measure of how large or dramatic an event sounds.

5. No numbers without a named source

No casualty figures and no dollar figures are published without a named original source. Numbers travel fast and are hard to correct once they spread, so they do not go up on a rumor.

Verified and unverified are kept separate

Verified reports go in the main feed. Unverified claims are kept in a separate section with a dashed-amber border, labeled Unverified, with a line explaining: “Why this is unverified.”

That separation matters. An unverified claim is not hidden, and it is not promoted to fact either. It is shown for what it is, so you know it exists without mistaking it for confirmed news.

A concrete example: the tanker claims

Iran has claimed that tankers hit naval mines — up to six vessels since Sunday, Nov 16, 2026. Those claims remain unverified. They are kept in the Unverified section, separate from the verified feed.

That is different from the UKMTO-verified projectile strikes on tankers. Nine ships have been hit since Nov 1, including the fatal Nov 4 Velos Sapphire strike and the Nov 16 On Phoenix strike. Those strikes have original-source verification, so they are in the main feed.

Same waterway, same week, two different standards of evidence — and two different places on the site. That is the method working as intended.

Why this is unverified — the pattern to watch:

A claim from one side, with no confirmation from an original source such as UKMTO or JMIC, stays unverified until that confirmation exists — no matter how many times it is reposted.

How to run the same checks yourself

  1. Check the original source, not the aggregator. Find where the report first came from. If a post does not name or link an original source, treat it as unconfirmed.
  2. Look for attribution. Who is actually making this claim? A contested claim with no named source is a warning sign.
  3. Watch for unverified claims presented as fact. Confident wording is not evidence. Look for the confidence level and the sourcing behind it.
  4. Beware of single-source viral claims. If only one account is saying it and everyone else is just repeating that account, it is still a single source.

These four checks take under a minute, and they filter out most of the false or overstated conflict news that spreads during a fast-moving story.
