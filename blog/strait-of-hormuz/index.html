Strait of Hormuz: Why It Matters and What Happens If Iran Closes It

The Strait of Hormuz is a narrow waterway between Iran and Oman — and the single most important oil route in the world. When tankers are attacked there, or Iran threatens to close it, oil markets, the US Navy, and governments in Asia all react at once. This post explains what the strait is, what is happening there now, and answers the questions people are actually asking about it.

The direct answer: No single country owns the Strait of Hormuz, Iran has never fully closed it, and as of NOV 17, 2026, shipping is still moving through it — even as a sustained campaign of attacks on tankers in and around the strait continues.

About a fifth of the world’s oil passes through the strait every day. That is why a threat to close it matters far beyond the Gulf, why the US keeps naval forces there, and why the verified reports from the strait are tracked live on the Conflict Ready USA dashboard.

What the Strait of Hormuz Is

The Strait of Hormuz connects the Persian Gulf to the Gulf of Oman and the Arabian Sea. Iran lies on its northern shore; Oman — and, at the western end, the United Arab Emirates — lies to the south. At its narrowest point the strait is only about 21 nautical miles wide, and the shipping lanes in each direction are just a few miles across.

Those lanes carry oil and liquefied natural gas from Saudi Arabia, Iraq, Kuwait, the UAE, Qatar, and Iran itself out to the world — most of it heading to Asia. There is no sea route around it for Gulf oil. Ships either go through Hormuz, or Gulf oil does not move by sea at all. That geography is the whole story: a very large share of the world’s energy, passing through a very narrow stretch of water, along the coast of a country that has repeatedly threatened to block it.

Frequently Asked Questions

Which country owns the Strait of Hormuz?

No single country owns it. The strait sits between Iran to the north and Oman (and the UAE) to the south, and the shipping lanes pass through the territorial waters of both Iran and Oman. Under international law, it is an international strait with a right of transit passage — ships from any country can pass through. Iran controls the northern shore and has repeatedly threatened to close it, but it does not own the strait. The geography is why the attacks in and around the strait involve more than one country’s waters.

Why is the Strait of Hormuz so important?

It is the world’s most important oil chokepoint. About a fifth of the world’s oil — roughly 17 to 20 million barrels a day — passes through it, mostly from Saudi Arabia, Iraq, Kuwait, the UAE, and Qatar heading to Asia. If the strait were blocked, global oil supply would drop sharply and prices would spike. It is also the route US Navy ships use to enter and leave the Persian Gulf, and it is where the current tanker attacks are happening.

How many ships go through the Strait of Hormuz?

Thousands of vessels a year go through the strait, including oil tankers carrying about 17–20 million barrels of oil a day — roughly 20% of global oil consumption. The strait is one of the busiest shipping lanes in the world. Since November 2026, the route has also been the site of a campaign of attacks on tankers — see the dashboard’s verified UKMTO reports.

Has Iran ever closed the Strait of Hormuz?

No. Iran has threatened to close it many times, but it has never fully closed the strait. What Iran has done is harass shipping, seize tankers, lay mines (according to US and UK reporting), and attack vessels — including the tanker campaign in November 2026. A full closure would be a major escalation, and it would cut off Iran’s own oil exports too.

Will Iran close the Strait of Hormuz?

There is no definitive answer. Iran has threatened closure repeatedly, and the IRGC has claimed at various points that the strait is closed — but those claims are unverified, and shipping continues to move through it. What we know: Iran has the capability to disrupt shipping (mines, fast boats, missiles), and it has been doing so, as the verified attacks on the dashboard show. Whether it will attempt a full closure is a decision only Tehran can make, and it has not done so. What is not known is whether that changes — no verified reporting establishes a decision in Tehran to close the strait.

Can Iran close the Strait of Hormuz legally?

No, not under international law. The strait is an international waterway, and the UN Convention on the Law of the Sea (UNCLOS) guarantees transit passage — the right of ships from any country to pass through. Iran is a party to UNCLOS. A closure would violate that right and would almost certainly draw a military response from the US and its allies, who have made clear they will keep the strait open — the posture behind the US response tracked on the dashboard.

What has happened in the Strait of Hormuz?

Since November 2026, there has been a sustained campaign of attacks on commercial tankers in and around the strait and the Gulf of Oman. The dashboard has verified reports of multiple tanker strikes (UKMTO warnings 196-26 through 210-26), including the first confirmed fatality (Velos Sapphire, Nov 4), the first crew evacuation (Nov 7), and a tanker hit twice (Nov 17). Iran has also claimed mine strikes on vessels, though the number and details are disputed. The US has responded with limited strikes on Iranian targets, including on Qeshm Island.

How much oil does the US get from the Strait of Hormuz?

Very little. The United States produces most of its own oil and imports the bulk of the rest from Canada and Mexico. Only a small fraction of US oil imports pass through the Strait of Hormuz. The countries most dependent on Hormuz oil are in Asia — China, India, Japan, and South Korea. That said, a closure would still hit the US, because it would spike global oil prices and disrupt the world economy — the same price shock tracked alongside the dashboard reporting.

How much oil does the US get from Iran?

Essentially none. The United States does not buy oil from Iran. US sanctions prohibit Iranian oil imports, and there has been no legal US purchase of Iranian crude in years. Iran’s oil goes almost entirely to China.

Who buys oil from Iran?

China is by far the largest buyer of Iranian oil, taking the overwhelming majority of Iran’s exports. Smaller volumes go to a handful of other countries, often through intermediaries. Iran sells its oil at a discount because of sanctions, which is why China — the world’s largest oil importer — is willing to buy it.

Does Iran have a navy?

Yes. Iran has two navies: the regular navy (IRIN) and the naval arm of the Islamic Revolutionary Guard Corps (IRGC Navy). The IRGC Navy operates fast attack boats, mines, and coastal missiles in the Persian Gulf and the Strait of Hormuz, and it is the force behind the harassment and attacks on shipping tracked on the dashboard. The regular navy operates larger ships further out, including in the Gulf of Oman and beyond.

Why is the Strait of Hormuz so important to Saudi Arabia?

Almost all of Saudi Arabia’s oil exports by sea pass through the Strait of Hormuz. Saudi Arabia has built a bypass — the East-West Pipeline, which carries oil from the Gulf coast to the Red Sea port of Yanbu — but the pipeline can only handle part of its exports (around 4–5 million barrels a day at full capacity, versus total exports of roughly 6–7 million). The Houthis claimed in November 2026 to have launched drones at two pumping stations on the East-West Pipeline — a claim tracked on the dashboard — which underscores why the pipeline is itself a target.

Does Iran have nuclear weapons?

No verified evidence shows Iran has a nuclear weapon. Iran has a nuclear program and has enriched uranium to levels that concern the IAEA and Western governments, but there is no confirmed report that Iran has built or tested a nuclear weapon. The IAEA monitors Iran’s nuclear facilities, and the dashboard tracks IAEA reporting on the program. What is known is the enrichment activity the IAEA reports; what is not established is a weapon — and this post does not present one as existing without that confirmation.

Where did Iran bomb the US?

Iran has fired drones at US forces in Bahrain — three times in five days in November 2026, targeting a US radar installation at Sheikh Isa Air Base. US Central Command confirmed the drones were intercepted with no damage to US equipment and no injuries — see the Bahrain reports on the dashboard. Iran has also attacked commercial shipping in the Gulf (tankers), which the US treats as a threat to its interests and allies. Iran has not bombed the US homeland.

What will happen to oil prices if Iran closes the Strait of Hormuz?

Prices would almost certainly rise, and sharply. About 20% of the world’s oil passes through the strait, and there is no way to replace that volume quickly. How high prices go depends on how long a closure lasts and whether other producers (Saudi Arabia, the US) can increase output or release reserves. The exact price impact is uncertain — it depends on the duration, the market’s reaction, and whether the closure is total or partial. But every analyst agrees a closure would be a major shock to oil prices and the global economy. There is no definitive price figure anyone can honestly give in advance; what is known is the share of supply at risk, and what is not known is the duration that would decide the size of the spike.

Where does China get its oil?

China is the world’s largest oil importer, and it buys from many countries: Russia, Saudi Arabia, Iraq, Angola, Brazil, and Iran, among others. A large share of China’s oil passes through the Strait of Hormuz (from the Gulf producers and from Iran). China is also the largest buyer of Iranian oil. If the strait were closed, China would be one of the countries hit hardest, which is one reason Beijing has an interest in keeping it open.
